Self-study syllabus · three linked fields

Social, Behavioural
& Communication Psychology

One curriculum, three tracks. Social psychology explains how other people change what you think and do. Behavioural psychology explains how consequences change what you do next. Communication is the machinery both run on — and the one you can practise daily.

How to use this

Tick a line only after you can explain it out loud, without notes, to someone who hasn't read it. Recognition feels like learning and isn't. Every part ends with a Lab — a small real-world exercise. The labs are the point; the reading is scaffolding.

Lines marked core are the load-bearing ideas — if you only get 20% through, get those. Lines marked contested are famous findings that have failed, shrunk, or been reinterpreted since. Learn them and learn why they're disputed; that's the actual skill.

Track A

Social Psychology

Parts I–V

How the real, imagined, or implied presence of other people shapes thought, feeling and action. The field with the most spectacular findings — and the most spectacular replication failures, which is exactly why methods come first.

IFoundations & Methods3 topics

Before any content: how these fields decide what's true, and why a headline finding from 2005 might be worth nothing today. This part is short and non-negotiable — it's the filter you'll run every later claim through.

01What These Three Fields Are

  • 1.4 Where they overlap — persuasion, norms, habit, group decision-making

Behavioural Psychology

Parts VI–IX

📚 Core Reading

Textbooks — pick one per track

  • Aronson, Wilson, Sommers — Social Psychology · the standard undergraduate text; readable, good on classic studies.
  • Gilovich, Keltner, Nisbett — Social Psychology · a stronger alternative if you want more research reasoning.
  • Domjan — The Principles of Learning and Behavior · the serious learning-theory text; do the Rescorla–Wagner sections properly.
  • Miltenberger — Behavior Modification: Principles and Procedures · the applied companion; the functional-assessment chapters are the useful ones.
  • Adler, Rodman, du Pré — Understanding Human Communication · broad and practical.
  • Griffin — A First Look at Communication Theory · one theory per chapter; best for the theory map.

Trade books that are actually load-bearing

  • Cialdini — Influence · Part III's applied layer; read it, then check the effect sizes.
  • Kahneman — Thinking, Fast and Slow · excellent on judgment, unreliable on priming — read with Part I's filter on.
  • Stone, Patton & Heen — Difficult Conversations · the best practical book in Track C.
  • Fisher & Ury — Getting to Yes · short, and the interests/positions distinction is worth the whole book.
  • Miller & Rollnick — Motivational Interviewing · where Tracks B and C actually merge.
  • Wendy Wood — Good Habits, Bad Habits · habit science from the researcher, not the influencer.
  • Thaler & Sunstein — Nudge: The Final Edition · the revised edition; the original oversold several chapters.

On the evidence base itself

  • Open Science Collaboration (2015), Science · the replication paper. Read the actual figures.
  • Ritchie — Science Fictions · fraud, bias, negligence and hype, written by a psychologist.
  • Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan (2010), "The weirdest people in the world?" · one paper that recalibrates the whole field.
  • Le Texier (2019) on the Stanford Prison Experiment · read alongside 10.4.

Free courses & primary sources

  • Yale — Introduction to Psychology (Paul Bloom), free on Coursera/YouTube.
  • Simply Psychology & Noba Project · reliable summaries for orientation, never as your only source.
  • Original papers for every core line you can find them for. Textbook summaries of Milgram, Asch and Skinner are all subtly wrong.
A standing warning

These three fields have the widest gap in psychology between what is popularly repeated and what the evidence supports. Body language "tells", power posing, learning styles, the 7-38-55 rule, most of behavioural priming, the Stanford Prison Experiment, and the tidier versions of stereotype threat and implicit bias are all either dead or much smaller than advertised. If a claim about human behaviour is memorable, tidy, and has a number in it, assume it shrank — then go check.
