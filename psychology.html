⏳Studies That Shaped Psychologya timeline of turning points

Psychology moves in shocks — a study, a scandal, a patient. These are the moments that changed what the field believed, including the ones that changed it by being wrong.

1859 Darwin, On the Origin of Species▸

What: Mind became a product of evolution rather than a separate spiritual substance — which meant animals could be studied to learn about humans.

Impact: The intellectual permission slip for a science of behaviour. Comparative psychology, functionalism and evolutionary psychology all trace back here.

→ 2.1 · 8.4

1879 Wundt opens the first laboratory▸

What: At Leipzig, Wundt began measuring reaction times and trained introspection under controlled conditions.

Impact: Psychology's official birthday — the moment it separated from philosophy by adopting the experiment.

→ 2.2

1885 Ebbinghaus & the forgetting curve▸

What: Using nonsense syllables on himself, Ebbinghaus plotted how fast memory decays and how relearning speeds up.

Impact: Proved memory could be measured quantitatively — and produced the spacing effect, still the single most useful finding for studying.

→ 16.1 · 16.7

1890 James's Principles of Psychology▸

What: Two volumes covering habit, emotion, attention, the self and the "stream of consciousness" — still readable today.

Impact: Defined the questions the field would spend the next century on, and founded functionalism: ask what a mental process is for.

→ 2.3 · 22.2

1900 Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams▸

What: The unconscious, repression and dream symbolism presented as a systematic theory of mind.

Impact: Scientifically weak, culturally enormous. Gave us the unconscious, defence mechanisms, childhood's importance and the very idea of talking therapy.

→ 2.4 · 27.1 · 35.1

1904–27 Pavlov's dogs▸

What: A physiologist studying digestion noticed dogs salivating at the sound of the assistant, and systematically mapped conditioned reflexes.

Impact: Classical conditioning — the first demonstration that learning follows discoverable laws. Still the basis of exposure therapy.

→ 13.1 · 35.3

1913 · 1920 Watson's manifesto & Little Albert▸

What: Watson declared psychology should study only observable behaviour, then conditioned an infant to fear a white rat.

Impact: Launched behaviourism's 40-year dominance — and became the standard example of research ethics before there were any.

→ 2.5 · 13.3 · 3.8

1932 Bartlett's Remembering▸

What: English participants retelling a Native American folk tale steadily distorted it toward their own cultural expectations.

Impact: Memory is reconstructive, not reproductive — the finding that underwrites everything from false memory to eyewitness reform.

→ 16.3 · 16.4

1938–53 Skinner's operant chamber▸

What: Skinner showed behaviour is shaped by its consequences, and mapped the schedules of reinforcement precisely.

Impact: Operant conditioning — the most reliably replicated body of findings in psychology, and the engine behind every slot machine and notification badge.

→ 13.4 · 13.6 · 38.3

1953 Patient H.M.▸

What: Surgery for epilepsy removed Henry Molaison's medial temporal lobes. He could no longer form new conscious memories — but could still learn new motor skills.

Impact: Proved memory is not one system: explicit and implicit memory are separable, and the hippocampus is required for one but not the other.

→ 16.5 · 15.6 · 7.1

1956–59 The cognitive revolution▸

What: Miller's "magical number seven", Newell & Simon's problem-solving programs, and Chomsky's demolition of Skinner's Verbal Behavior, all within three years.

Impact: The mind was readmitted to psychology as an information-processing system — the framework almost all modern research still uses.

→ 2.6 · 15.3 · 19.3

1958–69 Harlow's monkeys & Bowlby's attachment theory▸

What: Infant monkeys chose a cloth "mother" over the one that fed them; Bowlby argued attachment is a biological need, not a learned by-product of feeding.

Impact: Rewrote childcare, hospital visiting rules and adoption policy worldwide. Comfort and security are primary needs.

→ 25.5 · 25.6

1961–63 Bandura's Bobo doll & Milgram's obedience studies▸

What: Children copied aggression they had merely watched; ordinary adults delivered what they believed were lethal shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to.

Impact: Observational learning broke behaviourism's monopoly; Milgram made situationism — and research ethics — impossible to ignore. Both are heavily re-examined today.

→ 14.3 · 30.2 · 3.8

1971–73 Zimbardo's prison study & Rosenhan's pseudopatients▸

What: A simulated prison was halted early amid abuse; healthy volunteers reporting one hallucination were admitted to psychiatric hospitals and diagnosed as schizophrenic.

Impact: Together they hammered situational power and diagnostic unreliability into public consciousness — and both have since been shown to be far weaker evidence than the textbooks claimed. Keep both facts.

→ 30.3 · 32.5 · 4.6

1973–79 Tversky & Kahneman on heuristics and biases▸

What: A series of elegant demonstrations that human judgement departs from probability theory in systematic, predictable ways; prospect theory followed in 1979.

Impact: Created behavioural economics, won a Nobel prize, and reshaped policy, medicine and finance. The bias literature has since had its own replication reckoning.

→ 18.1 · 18.3 · 18.6

1973 · 1980 DSM-II drops homosexuality; DSM-III arrives▸

What: Homosexuality was removed as a disorder in 1973; in 1980 DSM-III replaced psychoanalytic descriptions with explicit symptom checklists, and added PTSD.

Impact: Diagnosis became reliable but contested, and it became undeniable that "disorder" is a category with a social and political history.

→ 32.2 · 32.5 · 33.3

1974–2000s Loftus & the memory wars▸

What: Loftus showed leading questions alter eyewitness memory, and that entirely false childhood memories can be implanted.

Impact: Overturned "recovered memory" therapy, changed police interviewing and courtroom rules, and made memory's unreliability a legal issue.

→ 16.4 · 37.3

1990s The Decade of the Brain & fMRI▸

What: Functional imaging made it possible to watch a living brain during a task, and funding poured into neuroscience.

Impact: Created cognitive neuroscience — and a decade of overclaiming that the field is still correcting, from "the God spot" to blob-based headlines.

→ 7.3 · 7.5

2010 Henrich: the WEIRD problem▸

What: A review showed most published findings came from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic samples — often undergraduates — yet were reported as human universals.

Impact: Forced psychology to ask who "people" means in its own conclusions, and drove the growth of cross-cultural replication.

→ 3.5 · 31.7 · 39.1

2011–15 The replication crisis breaks▸

What: A journal published evidence for precognition using standard methods; Simmons et al. showed those same methods could "prove" anything; then the Reproducibility Project replicated only ~36% of 100 studies.

Impact: The most important thing to happen to psychology in your lifetime. Killed several famous effects, and produced preregistration, registered reports, open data and bigger samples.

→ 4.6 · 4.7 · 4.8

Now Reform, big teams, psychedelics & AI▸

What: Many-labs collaborations, a rebuilt evidence base, renewed clinical trials of psychedelics, and language models being used both as research tools and as objects of study.

Impact: The chapters being written right now — and the reason it's worth learning methods first: they're what let you judge all of it.

→ 39.4 · 36.3 · 38.4

🎯 How to Apply What You Learn

Reading is input. Applying is where it sticks. After a module, pick one of these and do it — with your notes closed.
The Learning Loop — psychology's own findings say the last two stages do nearly all the work.
1 · Absorb2 · Organise3 · Apply ★4 · Retrieve ★

🗣️ 1. Explain it — the fastest gap-finder

  • Feynman technique — explain the concept in plain language as if to a 12-year-old. Where you stumble is your gap.
  • Teach it to Claude — "I'll explain why negative reinforcement isn't punishment; poke holes in it."
  • Write a mini-explainer — one paragraph, from memory, no notes open.
  • e.g. Explain "why correlation doesn't imply causation" using a real headline.

🔬 2. Interrogate the study — the core skill of this subject

  • The four questions: Who were the participants? What exactly was manipulated or measured? How big was the effect? Did it replicate?
  • Design it yourself — before reading the method, decide how you would test the claim. Then compare.
  • Find the confound — every study has one. Naming it is the whole exam skill.
  • Do this on one classic study per Part and you'll never read a psychology claim naively again.

👀 3. Observe it in the wild

  • Spot the concept — find one real instance this week of the thing you just learnt: an anchoring effect in a shop, a variable-ratio schedule in an app, a fundamental attribution error in an argument.
  • Keep a bias diary — one entry a day. Nothing teaches Part VI faster than catching yourself.
  • Predict, then check — guess what the study found before you read the result.

🧠 4. Run it on yourself (carefully)

  • Self-experiment — apply spacing and retrieval practice to this very file, and track whether it works.
  • Behaviour change — pick one habit and design a proper reinforcement schedule and cue for it (Part IV + 37.4).
  • Caution: reading about disorders is not diagnosing yourself. If something here resonates uncomfortably, that's a reason to talk to a GP, not to conclude anything.

⚖️ 5. Argue both sides — deep processing

  • Steelman the rival approach — argue the behaviourist case, then the cognitive one, as each would argue it themselves.
  • Socratic sparring with Claude — "Debate me on whether the Stanford Prison Experiment tells us anything. Take the opposite side."
  • Name the assumption — every psychological theory rests on one about human nature. Find it.

🧩 6. Connect & compare — builds schemas

  • Link levels — take one behaviour and explain it biologically, cognitively, socially and developmentally.
  • Build comparison tables from memory — the therapies, the memory stores, the attachment styles, the schools.
  • Concept-map a whole Part on one page.

✏️ 7. Test yourself — the highest-yield habit

  • Write your own questions as you read (that's what the 🧠 Recall column in each lesson is for), then answer them cold the next day.
  • Space it — revisit a module after 1 day, 1 week, 1 month.
  • Interleave — mix modules from different Parts so you have to choose the right concept, not just apply the last one you read.

⭐ Best three to start with

  • Interrogate one classic study per Part using the four questions.
  • Explain the module out loud in 60 seconds with everything closed.
  • Write your own quiz questions — they double as your spaced-retrieval bank.
